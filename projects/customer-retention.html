The business question

Churn risk identifies customers who may leave. A paid offer has to clear a second test. The value of keeping the customer, adjusted for the chance the offer works, has to cover its cost. A broad campaign can spend on customers who would have stayed anyway, or pay more for an offer than the relationship is worth.

My contribution

I built the customer-level analysis in Python and SQL, estimated profit-adjusted lifetime value, and trained and validated the churn model. I used those inputs to design the offer screen, simulate budget tradeoffs, and specify how a controlled test would measure incremental value.

What the simulation showed

I tested how well the model ranked risk after removing direct cancellation signals, then estimated net save value by subtracting intervention cost from the expected value saved. Under the base assumptions, 7.36% of scored customers cleared the paid-offer screen.

Targeting all positive-value candidates produced an estimated $15.83 million in net save-value proxy at 20.0× modeled paid-offer ROI. The ROI expresses modeled net save value per dollar of estimated offer cost.

Where to set the budget

The $750K point captured 99.99% of the maximum net value across the budgets tested in the simulation. I used that point to size a proposed experiment with 62,836 eligible customers and stratified treatment and control groups.

The decision to scale would depend on lower churn, positive incremental net value, and customer-experience measures meeting the test’s criteria.

What the experiment would establish

Response, lifetime value, and offer cost are model inputs. The financial figures describe a planning scenario. A randomized holdout would show how many customers stayed because of the offer, giving the team a basis for deciding whether to scale, revise, or stop the program.
