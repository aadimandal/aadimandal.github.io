Learning on the ground

At UC Riverside, I studied quantitative economics and econometrics and competed as an NCAA Division I sprinter. In class, I learned to ask what the evidence could explain. On the track, I learned to pay attention to how consistently I could execute a plan. I needed both habits when I began managing campus recreation.

I supervised 15–20 student employees across 8–12 leagues and open recreation blocks each week. When late starts kept recurring, I built Excel and R reports so we could see where staffing and the schedule were falling out of sync. I recommended format changes and supported the team’s staffing and scheduling adjustments. Over two quarters, the recreation operation cut late starts by 20% and maintained 95%+ on-time game completion.

I used the same approach in the School of Business, where an Excel tracker showed fee-deadline traffic could reach two to three times normal volume. I recommended changes to staffing and service-window assignments. The operation reduced peak waits by about 25% during high-demand periods and cut corrections by about 15% per 1,000 transactions.

From a forecast to a plan

In Purdue’s Industry Practicum, I work on demand forecasting and use optimization to compare staffing plans. I’m interested in what happens when a forecast has to become a schedule. Who is available to work? What coverage can the team provide? How much does the plan depend on the forecast being right?

Through a project-based business analyst internship with The Global Career Accelerator, I evaluated cross-sell opportunities in banking data and looked at how discounting affected gross margins. My independent work goes deeper into retention economics and credit policy. I use Python and SQL to make it possible to rerun the analysis and Tableau to show how the recommendation changes when assumptions move.
